# The Sawtooth Chart Was Never the Enemy

**TRXL 237 — Phil Read, co-founder and CEO, Read | Thomas**
Publication Date: 2026-08-17

## Website Excerpt (chosen)
Doing the work badly and getting corrected is how architects acquired judgment. That process is being automated, and almost nobody is talking about what replaces it. Phil Read on AI as an intern, and the interns who now have interns.

**Who this episode is for:** Especially relevant if you lead a practice, run design technology, or teach, and you're watching the tasks that used to season young architects get absorbed by software.

## Summary
Phil Read came back on the show (TRXL 237) for what's turning into an annual conversation. He's the co-founder and CEO of Read | Thomas, he was around Revit in its earliest days, and he spends most of the year sitting with firms in the States and Australia. That travel is the reason I keep asking him back. He sees what firms are doing with BIM, visualization, and AI, all which bear little resemblance to what gets posted on LinkedIn about them.

He opened by rehabilitating a diagram most of us were taught to treat as a failure. The sawtooth, the jagged line in the MacLeamy curve that BIM was supposed to smooth into something continuous, is in Phil's reading the most valuable part of the process. The jagged bits are where somebody with experience stops and says wait a minute.

From there we got into what AI is genuinely good at right now, which turns out to be materials and lighting studies rather than generating building form. We got into why automated documentation is fast but not yet trustworthy. And we spent a good stretch on if delegation is the skill that AI demands, and nobody teaches architects to delegate until roughly year twenty, what exactly are we preparing people for?

## Episode Analysis

### The Sawtooth Chart Was Never the Enemy
If you've sat through any BIM presentation in the last two decades you've seen the diagram, and you were told the jagged traditional line was the problem to be solved. Phil's position now is close to the opposite.

> "These little stopping points are where you inject judgment and experience."

His argument is that a genuinely continuous, frictionless process removes something you want. If the work simply flows from sketch to model to documentation without anyone being forced to stop, then nobody is forced to stop.

> "The sawtooth is where we stop and apply judgment and where we start over elegantly, as long as you can start over elegantly."

What convinced him wasn't theory. He was in Sydney with a firm using AI in early design, and someone asked how they'd get the AI output back into Revit. Phil told them they'd have to model it from scratch. He expected pushback. Instead the lead designer said that was fine, that's what he wanted to do anyway.

> "I don't want all that chaff. I want the essence of the idea, and then I want to apply judgment."

I pushed on this, because I think people want it both ways. They want the tool to do it for them, and they complain about having to fix the output. They also want total control, and a perfect result. You don't get both.

> "It doesn't do what we want. It does what we tell it or ask it."

### Done, But Not Finished
The clearest example in the conversation is a real hospital project with 2,000 patient rooms. Every room needs a ceiling plan, a plan, four elevations, two orthographic views, and a room data sheet. Nobody sets that up by hand. The firm is using Glyph to generate the sheets in minutes.

Then somebody has to open all 2,000 and check them.

> "The thing about these kinds of tools is it's done, but it's not finished. You have to go and check it."

The failure isn't speed, it's predictability. Phil names the specific gap that makes it exhausting: when you correct one instance, the tool doesn't ask whether it should correct the others.

> "But these automation tools don't do that. It just does everything. It vomits it all out."

Where he's genuinely enthusiastic is somewhere I didn't expect: studying materials, finishes, and light on top of a design that already has judgment applied to it. Speculative optioneering that used to take a week now takes a morning, and tenant-fit-out work that used to burn three people for two weeks, roughly twenty-five thousand dollars, before anyone knew whether the deal was alive.

### The Interns Are Getting Interns
Phil calls AI an architectural intern, and he means it structurally, not as a put-down.

> "What I don't think we're used to right now is the interns having interns."

The junior person who used to do the work can now delegate it. Which is fine, except doing the work is how people used to acquire judgment in the first place. Phil's answer is to move the skill earlier rather than mourn it.

> "We don't want to wait 20 years to learn how to delegate or learn how to ask good questions."

Architecture school is built on doing the reps yourself. Phil isn't arguing against it, but he wants studios teaching delegation and accountability alongside the reps.

> "Now be accountable for it. Now actually make the pieces and parts, create the instructions that would lead to constructability. Don't just make an image and that's where you stop."

The cost of getting this wrong showed up in a line Phil quoted from someone else:

> "I used to feel like I had to do 10 things in a day, and now I feel like I have to do 100. I think I'm starting to hate my job."

That's what happens when recovered time defaults to backlog. Phil's alternative is to spend it on speculative and pursuit work that was previously too expensive to attempt, where failing fast is the point.

### No More Than 50 People
The conversation closes on Phil's own event, and it runs on the same idea as everything else in this one: friction, kept on purpose.

He and Adam started it as two separate things, a leadership event in the fall and a technical event in the spring, until the two kept spilling into each other. This year they're combined, at a camp in North Florida with a spring-fed lake that runs about 80 degrees. September 27 through October 2, capped at 50 people.

> "What Adam and I aspired to do was create an environment where we could have quieter conversation, thoughtful conversations, good food, good discussions."

The "leadership" label undersells who it's for. Phil built the event around a pattern he kept noticing: you get good at the software, you get promoted into managing people, and the skills that got you there don't transfer.

> "I could always wrangle Revit to do what I wanted it to do. Trial and error with people you're delegating to doesn't work."

No alcohol, a French chef, family-style meals at one long table, and a three-hour gap built into every afternoon by design. Harlan Brumm, the Revit product manager at Autodesk, is the review Phil keeps coming back to:

> "He was standing on the lodge deck, rocking on his heels, and he said, 'We need more of this.'"

I've been. It's an unplugged feeling event. The name has nothing to do with architectural acoustics — it's a nod to unplugged sets, not the plugged-in industry events everybody already goes to. $999, all in, at aecacoustics.com.

## Conclusion
Judgment has to happen somewhere, and most of our current enthusiasm is aimed at removing exactly the moments where it used to happen. The sawtooth. The week of optioneering. The sheet an intern set up badly and got corrected on.

I don't think the answer is to slow down on principle. But there's a difference between compressing work and deleting the conditions that produce expertise, and right now we're doing both without distinguishing between them.

## What I Didn't Say on the Show
An intern is a person who is becoming something, and the becoming happens through work that a more experienced person could have done faster themselves. Firms tolerate the inefficiency because it manufactures the next generation of judgment. If the silicon intern does that work instead, the efficiency case is obvious and the succession case collapses, and nobody is going to notice for about ten years.

## My Question for You
When your team recovered time this year, where did that time go? Not where you intended it to go. Where did it end up?

## Practical Recommendation
Pick one pursuit you would have declined on cost and run it AI-first, with a hard time box of one morning. Then add one line to your next project kickoff: name who is accountable for reviewing AI-generated output, and what "checked" means for that deliverable.
